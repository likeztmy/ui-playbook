## The rule

**A wait is judged by how it feels, not how long it is.** The same second can pass unnoticed or drag, depending on what the screen does with it. Sometimes the fix is to show more: a skeleton, the work being done. Sometimes it's to show less: nothing at all for a quick request, or the result before the server has answered.

## Three limits

| Time | What it feels like | What it wants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 0.1 s | Instant: the user did it | Nothing but the result |
| Under 1 s | A pause, but the thought holds | Nothing, or a quiet sign that appears late |
| Up to 10 s | Attention starts to drift | A spinner or skeleton; for long, known work, progress |
| Over 10 s | The user goes elsewhere | A percent-done bar, a time left, and a way to cancel |

The thresholds are [NN/g's](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/), unchanged since Miller (1968) and Card et al. (1991).

## When it doesn't apply

- **The wait is the point.** A countdown before an irreversible send is a chance to stop it, and should be seen in full.
- **Honesty about failure.** Showing a result early is fine only if a failure is shown just as clearly when it comes.

## Where the playbook applies it

- [Button](/components/button): `delay-loading` before the spinner, and the label's width reserved so nothing shifts.
- [Toast](/components/toast): Undo after the action, instead of a confirmation before it.
- [Nothing shifts](/principles/nothing-shifts): a skeleton holds the space its content will take.
