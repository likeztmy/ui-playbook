# Colour and unread: what guides the eye, what the references do, which colour roles to use

Research for the colour system of **Post** (the demo mail app) and its unread/read list row. Today unread is shown only by weight (600 vs 400) on sender, subject and time, and the owner finds read and unread hard to tell apart at a glance. The directions to compare are an accent dot (Apple Mail), read rows receding onto a tinted background (Gmail), and an accent edge bar plus coloured subject (Outlook). Read with `2026-10-26-layout-and-guidance.md` (topic 1 covers common region and uniform connectedness; topic 4 covers "grey is still read"). Neither is repeated here.

**Verified** means the claim was read in the source named: full text where a PDF was opened, abstract only where marked. **Unverified** claims stay off pages.

## 1. Which attributes guide attention, and how strongly

- **Mechanism.** Attention can be steered before reading only by a small set of attributes. A target pops out when it differs from its neighbours on one of them by a margin far above the just-noticeable difference, and when the neighbours are uniform. Weight is not on the list by name. Presence of a feature is generally found faster than its absence, but that rule has caveats.
- **The list.**
  - Wolfe & Horowitz, *What attributes guide the deployment of visual attention and how do they do it?*, Nature Reviews Neuroscience 5:495–501, 2004, doi:10.1038/nrn1411 (https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/WolfeHorowitzNRN04.pdf, full text read). Table 1, "undoubted" attributes: colour, motion, orientation, size ("including length and spatial frequency"). "Probable": luminance onset (flicker), **luminance polarity**, vernier offset, stereoscopic depth and tilt, pictorial depth cues, **shape**, line termination, closure, topological status, curvature. Font, weight and boldness do not appear anywhere in the paper.
  - Wolfe & Horowitz, *Five factors that guide attention in visual search*, Nature Human Behaviour 1:0058, 2017, doi:10.1038/s41562-017-0058 (https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/FiveFactors_Wolfe-Horowitz_2017.pdf, full text read). Box 1 keeps the same undoubted four (size now adds "apparent size"). "There are probably only two dozen attributes that can guide attention." The five factors: bottom-up salience, top-down feature guidance, scene structure and meaning, search history, and value. Salience "increases with difference from the distractors … and with the homogeneity of the distractors". Search efficiency "is dependent on the number of features shared by targets and distractors", and observers can guide to several target features at once.
- **How strong, in numbers (Wolfe & Horowitz 2004).** Pop-out search has a slope near 0 ms per item. Searching for a 2 among 5s costs "about 20–40 ms per item" when the target is present, "a bit more than twice that steep" when absent. Search paced by eye movements costs 150–300 ms per item. No single slope cut-off separates guided from unguided search. For colour, efficient search needs a target–distractor difference "much greater than the just noticeable difference", and a colour target flanked in colour space by distractors on both sides is found inefficiently.
- **History and value (Wolfe & Horowitz 2017).** Priming of pop-out "takes about 200 ms to develop" and "can last for at least a week". Value learned for a colour still biases attention "half a year after acquisition". A cue used the same way on every row gets more effective over time; a colour reused for other meanings loses that history.
- **Classic origin.** Treisman & Gelade, *A feature-integration theory of attention*, Cognitive Psychology 12:97–136, 1980, doi:10.1016/0010-0285(80)90005-5. **Primary not read** (no abstract in any open index). Wolfe & Horowitz 2017 summarise it: Treisman "proposed that targets defined by unique features would pop out of displays".
- **Search asymmetry: presence beats absence.**
  - Treisman & Souther, *Search asymmetry: a diagnostic for preattentive processing of separable features*, J. Exp. Psychol. General 114:285–310, 1985, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.114.3.285 (abstract read, Europe PMC PMID 3161978). Finding a circle with an intersecting line "is independent of the number of regular circles", while finding the plain circle among circles with lines "increases linearly with the number of distractors". The pattern suggests parallel search when the target has a unique feature, and serial search when it is marked only by "the absence of a feature that is present in all the distractors". Colours, line ends and closure behaved as features.
  - Wolfe & Horowitz 2004: "for many attributes, the presence of a property is more readily detected than its absence". The asymmetry only counts "if the easy search is efficient".
  - Rosenholtz, *Search asymmetries? What search asymmetries?*, Perception & Psychophysics 63:476–489, 2001, doi:10.3758/BF03194414 (abstract read, PMID 11414135): many reported asymmetries "contain built-in design asymmetries", and a plain target–distractor similarity model predicts them. No 2015+ primary replication on UI-like displays was found.
- **Bold and weight as a search cue.**
  - Strobelt, Oelke, Kwon, Schreck & Pfister, *Guidelines for Effective Usage of Text Highlighting Techniques*, IEEE TVCG 22(1):489–498, 2016, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2015.2467759 (full text read via the Harvard VCG copy). 45 analysed crowd workers (63 recruited) clicked as many highlighted words as possible in 13 s, in dense text. Normalised scores: font size 0.86, border 0.84, yellow background 0.78, red text 0.76, **bold 0.74**, shadow 0.71, underline 0.58, letter spacing 0.55, italic 0.15. F(8, 801) = 171.6, p < .0001. "Increasing font size works best as a single highlighting technique." The Tukey rank-group letters were garbled in extraction, so which neighbouring pairs differ significantly is **unverified**. The authors' hypothesis: background scored above red text "because the colored area is much larger". They state it as a hypothesis and did not test it.
  - Doi, *Effective highlighting modes of graphical user interfaces in visual information search*, Universal Access in the Information Society 22:111–119, 2023 (online 2021), doi:10.1007/s10209-021-00827-x (author manuscript read, Okayama repository). 38 participants searched spaced GUI words (12 or 21 on screen, 4 highlighted). Highlight type affected RT, F(2, 74) = 4.02, p = 0.022. Bold gave significantly faster RT than red or red + bold. Red alone gave significantly lower accuracy, χ²(2) = 7.95, p = 0.019. The per-condition RT values sit in tables that did not extract and are **unverified**. The weight values used are not stated.
  - Reading the two together: bold works roughly as well as a colour change but no better than size. It isn't a named guiding attribute. A plausible route is that bold text is darker and thicker (luminance and spatial frequency), but no source tests that, so it is **unverified**.
- **Implications for Post.**
  - 600 vs 400 is a small difference in a cue that isn't a basic feature. Wolfe's rule (the difference must sit far above threshold, among uniform distractors) predicts that it won't pop out, which matches the owner's complaint. Keep weight as the reading cue, and add a cue on a guiding attribute: a colour mark, or a luminance difference big enough to see.
  - Put the mark on **unread** (the target, the one you look for), not on read. That follows Treisman & Souther, with Rosenholtz's caution that the edge comes from target–distractor difference rather than presence as such.
  - Use the accent only for unread in the list. Every other use of the same hue in rows (links, tags, selection) adds heterogeneous distractors and spends the priming history.

## 2. A large tinted area or a small mark

- **Mechanism.** Two separate effects. Area: a larger patch is easier to detect in peripheral vision, and red–green colour fades fastest away from the fixation point. Grouping: a uniform tint across rows binds those rows into one region, which is a grouping cue in its own right (see layout research, topic 1: Palmer & Rock 1994, Palmer 1992).
- **Findings.**
  - Hansen, Pracejus & Gegenfurtner, *Color perception in the intermediate periphery of the visual field*, Journal of Vision 9(4):26, 2009, doi:10.1167/9.4.26 (abstract read, Europe PMC). "Sensitivity to red-green color variations declines more steeply toward the periphery than sensitivity to luminance or blue-yellow colors." Chromatic detection "gets worse with increasing eccentricity but is still possible", using stimuli of variable size.
  - Strobelt et al. 2016, above: yellow background 0.78 vs red text 0.76, with the authors attributing the gap to area as a hypothesis. That is the only direct text comparison found, and the gap is small.
  - Wolfe & Horowitz 2004 list texture segmentation by colour as converging evidence that colour guides attention: regions of differently coloured elements separate effortlessly.
  - No study comparing a row-background tint with a row-leading dot for unread detection was found (search 2026-10-26). **The comparison is open; the demo has to answer it.**
- **What the tint direction changes.** In Gmail's pattern the tint goes on the *read* rows, the distractors, and unread rows are marked by the lack of tint. White against grey is still a luminance difference, so this isn't a strict absence search. But each unread row is a gap in a grey band rather than a mark of its own, and with many unread rows in a row the band breaks apart. This is inference from the sources above, not a tested claim.
- **Implications for Post.**
  - A tint is a weak cue unless its luminance step is large. A step large enough to see across a full row also re-groups the list (read rows bind into bands), which competes with date sections and selection fill. Check the tint against the selection and hover fills before choosing it.
  - A dot is small but sits in a fixed column at the row's left edge, where the eye returns on every line (layout research, topic 2). Prefer a blue-axis accent over red, since blue–yellow survives the periphery better than red–green (Hansen 2009).
  - Demo with one variable: the same 20-row inbox, with weight unchanged; only the added cue changes (none / dot / read-row tint / edge bar). Task arising from the content: "how many unread from Linear?"

## 3. What the reference products and design systems say

- **Apple Mail (macOS).** Mail User Guide, *Read and respond to emails in Mail on Mac* (https://support.apple.com/guide/mail/read-and-respond-to-emails-mlhlp1010/mac, read; version selector at macOS 27): "Email messages you haven't read yet have a blue dot or star (for messages from VIPs) next to them." Older guide (Mail 11, https://support.apple.com/guide/mail/mark-messages-as-read-or-unread-mail0025/11.0/mac/10.13): "When you select the message to read it, the blue dot disappears." No setting for the dot was found in the guide. Whether unread rows are also bold in Mail is **not documented**.
- **Apple Mail (iOS).** No iPhone User Guide page describing the unread dot was found. **Unverified from docs**, though the dot is visible in the product.
- **Gmail.** Help documents marking read and unread (answer 12516) and the inbox type "Unread first": "your inbox will be separated into two sections: 'Unread' at the top and 'Everything else' at the bottom" (https://support.google.com/mail/answer/18522, read). Themes: "For some themes, you have the option to change the text background" (answer 112508). **No Gmail Help page describes bold unread rows or a tinted background on read rows**, so both are observed product behaviour only and stay unverified.
- **Outlook.** Microsoft Support, *View only unread messages in Outlook*: "By default, unread messages appear in bold in the message list." *Conditional Formatting in classic Outlook for Windows*: the default Unread messages rule "makes unread messages appear bold in the message list". The blue bar and blue subject: Microsoft 365 Blog, *A Modern, People-First Inbox in Outlook 2013* (25 Sep 2012, J. Meisels): the team made "unread mails in your inbox stand out by using Outlook's new blue color", meaning blue text and a bar to the left of each unread message. Whether current new Outlook documents the bar is **unverified** (Microsoft Q&A threads only, excluded).
- **Material 3, Badges** (https://m3.material.io/components/badges/guidelines and /specs, read in browser). Badges "show notifications, counts, or status information on navigation items and icons". "A small badge is a simple circle, used to indicate an unread notification"; it "uses only shape to indicate a status change". Small badge 6dp; colour role **Error** / On error. Custom roles need "contrast of at least 3:1". The scope is navigation destinations, not list rows.
- **Material 3, Color roles** uses mail as its own example: "Primary colors are used for the most important elements, like a starred message and FAB to start a new message."
- **Apple HIG and Fluent 2** give no unread-indicator guidance (HIG *Color*, *Lists and tables*; Fluent 2 *Color*, read). The rules that bear on it are in section 4.
- **Implications for Post.**
  - Apple (dot, documented) and Outlook (blue text plus bar, documented in 2012, bold documented today) both keep bold *and* add a hue mark on the unread row. Bold alone is nobody's whole answer. Gmail's tint is undocumented, so treat it as the weakest reference.
  - A dot inherits Apple's meaning without explanation, and Material's small badge uses the same "simple circle" for unread. Use Post's accent rather than Material's Error red: in Post, red should keep its error meaning (section 4).

## 4. Colour roles for an app UI

- **Material 3, Color roles** (https://m3.material.io/styles/color/roles, read in browser). 26 standard roles in six groups: primary, secondary, tertiary, error, surface, outline.
  - Primary is "for important actions and elements needing the most emphasis"; use it "for the most prominent components across the UI, such as the FAB, high-emphasis buttons, and active states". Secondary is "for less prominent components". Tertiary is "for smaller elements that need special emphasis but don't require immediate attention, such as a badge or notification".
  - "Use caution when changing color roles for visual effect."
  - Surface is "for backgrounds and large, low-emphasis areas". Five surface container levels run from lowest to highest emphasis. "The most common combination … uses surface for a background area and surface container for a navigation area."
  - Outline variant is for "decorative elements, such as dividers"; don't use it "to create visual hierarchy or define the visual boundary of targets".
  - Role pairs "provide an accessible minimum 3:1 contrast".
- **Material 2, Dark theme** (https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme.html, read in browser). "Use dark grey – rather than black"; "The recommended dark theme surface color is #121212". "At higher levels of elevation, components express depth by displaying lighter surface colors." Primary colours are desaturated to pass "at least 4.5:1"; "Large surfaces use a dark surface color, with limited color accents". M3 replaces the elevation overlays with the surface container tones above.
- **Apple HIG, Color** (JSON source read). "Avoid using the same color to mean different things", "especially when you use it to help communicate information like status or interactivity". "Avoid redefining the semantic meanings of dynamic system colors." iOS backgrounds come in primary / secondary / tertiary for hierarchy. macOS app accent colours apply to "buttons, selection highlighting, and sidebar icons", and the user's own accent setting replaces the app's. "Avoid relying solely on color to differentiate between objects … or communicate essential information."
- **Apple HIG, Dark Mode.** Base and elevated background sets: "The base colors are dimmer, making background interfaces appear to recede, and the elevated colors are brighter". Contrast "no lower than 4.5:1"; for custom colours "strive for a contrast ratio of 7:1, especially in small text".
- **Fluent 2, Color** (https://fluent2.microsoft.design/color, read in browser). There are three palettes: neutral, shared, brand. "Use lighter neutrals on surfaces to highlight areas of primary focus." Shared colours: "Use shared colors sparingly". Semantic colours "should always convey important information"; "Don't use them for decoration". Brand: "Avoid overusing brand colors or using them on large surfaces as they can dilute a hierarchy". "Don't use color as the only way to communicate."
- **WCAG 2.2.**
  - SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (A): colour is not "the only visual means of conveying information"; add cues "such as shape or text". Sufficient technique G183: a colour difference counts as a distinction when it reaches 3:1 against the surrounding text.
  - SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) (AA): text at 4.5:1, large text (at least 18 pt, or 14 pt bold) at 3:1. Values are not rounded: "4.499:1 would not meet".
  - SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (AA): 3:1 "against adjacent color(s)" for visual information needed to identify components "and states". The Understanding page names selected and focused states; unread isn't named, but it is the same kind of state indicator.
- **APCA status.** Apple HIG *Accessibility* names APCA alongside WCAG as "two popular standards", but its Accessibility Inspector uses WCAG AA values. WCAG 3.0 Working Draft (10 Sep 2026, https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag-3.0/): "The contrast algorithm used in WCAG 3 is yet to be determined"; the draft assumes it "will include a size/weight factor". APCA is not named. Not normative anywhere.
- **Implications for Post.**
  - One accent, one meaning: the accent marks *state that needs you* (unread dot, primary action, focus and selection only if they can't be confused with unread). Red is reserved for errors and destructive actions. Stars and flags get their own hue or a glyph, never the accent.
  - Neutrals carry structure: list on surface, sidebar on surface container, the open message on the same or a brighter surface. In dark mode, use dark grey rather than #000, with raised layers lighter (Material, Apple base/elevated).
  - Contrast budget: the unread dot needs 3:1 against the row background in both themes and in the hover and selected fills (1.4.11). Read text keeps 4.5:1 (1.4.3); read rows shouldn't be greyed below it to make unread stand out.

## 5. Colour-vision deficiency

- **Prevalence.**
  - Birch, *Worldwide prevalence of red-green color deficiency*, JOSA A 29(3):313–320, 2012, doi:10.1364/JOSAA.29.000313 (abstract read, Europe PMC): about **8% of men and 0.4% of women** of European descent; 4–6.5% of men of Chinese and Japanese ethnicity.
  - Simunovic, *Colour vision deficiency*, Eye 24:747–755, 2010, doi:10.1038/eye.2009.251 (abstract): congenital CVD "affects as many as 8% of males and 0.5% of females".
  - Recent: Ireland schoolchildren, Clin. Exp. Optom. 2021, doi:10.1111/cxo.13072 (abstract): 8.3% of boys and 1.8% of girls (n = 1,626); deutan 4.8% of boys, protan 1.7%, tritan 0.5%. Israeli male recruits 2000–2020, Br. J. Ophthalmol. 2025, doi:10.1136/bjo-2023-324564 (abstract): 28,001 of 916,388 (3.06%).
- **Which pairs fail.** Apple HIG *Accessibility*: colour-blind people "may have particular difficulty with pairings such as red-green and blue-orange". The common deficiencies are red–green (Birch, the Ireland data). Blue against white or grey differs on the blue–yellow and luminance axes, which red–green deficiency spares. That is standard colour science, but no source read here states it for UI colours, so it is **unverified in this file**. The claim that protans see red as darker is also **unverified here**.
- **Implications for Post.**
  - Around 1 in 12 men can't rely on red vs green. A blue unread dot against neutral rows is the safer hue; a red unread badge (Material's Error default) would be harder to tell apart from red error marks in the same list.
  - A dot is a shape cue as well as a colour cue: its presence carries the meaning, so a dot plus bold passes 1.4.1 without a label. A hue-only cue (blue subject text, Outlook-style, or a faint tint) needs either 3:1 against the read state (G183) or a second cue such as weight.

## Ranked directions for the Post demo

1. **Dot plus bold** (Apple). The dot is a presence cue on the target, in a fixed left column, on the blue axis. It is documented first-party, matches Material's "simple circle" for unread, and passes 1.4.1 as shape. Needs a 3:1 check on hover and selection fills.
2. **Edge bar plus coloured subject plus bold** (Outlook 2013). The cue is larger and also on the target, but hue spreads into text, which costs the one-accent rule and needs G183's 3:1 against read subjects.
3. **Read rows tinted** (Gmail). The cue goes on the distractors, its luminance step is usually small, and it re-groups the list. It has no first-party documentation. Test it, but expect it to lose.
