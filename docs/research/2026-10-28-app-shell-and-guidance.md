# App shell and guidance: sidebar, drafts, right-click, first run, titles, notifications

Written 2026-10-28 for **Track**, the Linear-style issue tracker demo: a sidebar (workspace, My issues, teams), view tabs All / Active / Backlog, a grouped list, a board, a side panel, ⌘K and a New issue composer. Read with `2026-10-25-topic-backlog.md` and `2026-10-26-layout-and-guidance.md`. Those files already cover these, and they aren't repeated here: Grossman 2007 hotkey learning, NN/g's hidden-navigation 27% vs 48%, NN/g's onboarding tutorials and the 20-second fade of coach marks, empty-state "pull revelations", the 3-click myth, Norman's signifiers, Gray et al.'s access cost, and the backlog entries *Command menu*, *Filter bar* (query-caused empty results), *Teach at the moment of need* and *Make the mode visible*. ## 1. Sidebar navigation and wayfinding

- **Mechanism.** A sidebar does two jobs at once: it says where you are, and it hints where each destination leads (*information scent*: Pirolli & Card, *Information Foraging*, Psychological Review 106(4):643–675, 1999, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643; citation checked, abstract not read). Both get worse with depth. Each extra level hides its children behind a guess, and the current-place mark can end up inside a closed group. This is where the platforms agree most strongly: three independent design systems give the same two-level limit.
- **Depth.**
  - Apple HIG *Sidebars* (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sidebars, JSON source read): "In general, show no more than two levels of hierarchy in a sidebar." Deeper data goes in "a split view interface that includes a content list between the sidebar items and detail view".
  - Fluent 2 *Nav* (https://fluent2.microsoft.design/components/web/react/core/nav/usage, read): "Navs can be organized with up to two levels of hierarchy."
  - Carbon *UI shell left panel* (https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/UI-shell-left-panel/usage/, read): "The left panel does not support three tiers of navigation."
  - Larson & Czerwinski, *Web Page Design: Implications of Memory, Structure and Scent for Information Retrieval*, CHI 98 (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/web-page-design-implications-memory-structure-scent-information-retrieval/, abstract read; the PDF is a scan with no text layer). Depth hurt search, and "a medium condition of depth and breadth outperformed the broadest shallow structure". So the rule isn't "flatten everything" either. **Their tested structures and times are unverified.**
- **Current place.**
  - Fluent: "The selection indicator tells people at a glance which page is active". And "If a nav sub-item is the active page and the category is closed, the selection indicator will display on the nav category." This is the counterintuitive detail: collapsing a group must not take away the "you are here" mark.
  - WAI-ARIA APG *Disclosure navigation* (https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/disclosure/examples/disclosure-navigation/, read): `aria-current="page"` "Indicates that the page referenced by the link is currently displayed", on exactly one link.
- **Labels vs icons.**
  - Fluent: "Whenever possible, use icons with nav category labels"; "Nav doesn't support an icon-only layout."
  - Majrashi, *Performance of mobile users with text-only and text-and-icon menus in seated and walking situations*, Behaviour & IT 41(1):32–50, 2022 (https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/tbitxx/v41y2022i1p32-50.html, abstract read; 2 × 18 participants). Text-and-icon medium-length menus gave faster selections than text-only menus when seated. When walking they were faster at both lengths and gave fewer wrong selections. Icons *with* labels beat labels alone. No icon-only condition was tested.
- **Showing and hiding.**
  - Apple: "Consider letting people hide the sidebar"; use "the platform-specific interactions they already know" (on macOS, a show/hide button or View-menu commands); "Avoid hiding the sidebar by default to ensure that it remains discoverable"; consider auto-collapsing it when the window narrows.
  - Fluent: the hamburger is "a persistent and predictable UX pattern to expand and collapse Nav", with the tooltips "Expand navigation" / "Collapse navigation". Neither Apple nor Fluent says where the toggle goes or whether the choice persists.
  - Material 3 Expressive deprecates the navigation drawer in favour of an expanded navigation rail. **This was seen only through secondary mirrors (SAP Fiori docs, 9to5Google), so it's unverified and stays off pages.**
- **What Linear does.**
  - Collapse with `[`, a click on the sidebar border, or "Collapse" in ⌘K. Expand with `[` again, the top-left sidebar icon, or ⌘K (https://linear.app/changelog/unpublished-collapsible-sidebar, dated 12 January 2023; the URL says "unpublished"). The collapse and expand controls sit in different places.
  - "Individual sections and teams in the sidebar can be collapsed" and "Linear will remember which sections the user collapsed to persist this information across sessions" (https://linear.app/changelog/2019-11-11-new-sidebar).
  - Since December 2024 you can reorder items, hide them behind *More*, and choose whether unread shows "with a count or dot" (https://linear.app/changelog/2024-12-18-personalized-sidebar).
- **Rule for Track.**
  - Level 1 holds workspace items: Inbox, My issues, then the Teams header. Level 2 holds each team's Issues / Active / Backlog views. All / Active / Backlog are tabs in the main area, not a third sidebar level.
  - Every row shows an icon and a label.
  - Collapsing hides the whole sidebar. There is no icon-only rail, because Fluent doesn't support one and Linear doesn't ship one.
  - The toggle is one button that stays at the same top-left coordinate in both states, plus `[`. Deviation from Linear, which collapses from the border but expands from the top-left icon: the control is in one place, which is the owner's rule.
  - Store the collapsed state and each open team in `localStorage`. Show the sidebar by default.
  - When a team is closed and one of its views is current, the selection fill moves to the team row.
- **Demo (one variable).** The same Track sidebar, changing only what happens to the current-place mark when its team is collapsed: the mark disappears, or it moves to the parent. Task from the content: "which team are you in?" after collapsing all teams.
- **Verdict.** Page-worthy as a principle or an app-shell component page. What makes it more than common knowledge: three systems independently agree on two levels, the mark moves up to the closed group, and icon plus label beats label alone (no icon-only condition was tested).

## 2. Never lose what someone typed

- **Mechanism.** People leave a composer in two ways, and they mean different things. *Incidental* leaving (clicking elsewhere, navigating, ⌘K to look something up) means "not now". *Deliberate* closing (Esc, ×) usually also means "not now", and only sometimes "throw this away". If both are treated as discard, the commonest exit destroys work. If both get a confirmation, every exit costs an extra question. The fix is to separate the two intents: leaving keeps the text, and discarding is its own command.
- **Strongest finding.**
  - Linear *Issues* (https://linear.app/docs/creating-issues, read). If you navigate away, "Linear will hide the issue modal and keep a temporary draft"; "The next time you go to create an issue, the editor will re-open with the previous content draft". That draft is local and is cleared by logging out, restarting or resetting. "If you use `Esc` or click on the close button, a pop-up modal will appear" offering to save it as a draft, which "persists across clients" and is listed on a Drafts page in the sidebar. "Drafts are stored for 6 months before being deleted automatically."
  - Gmail Help (https://support.google.com/mail/answer/9259768, read): "Drafts are saved automatically." Slack Help (https://slack.com/help/articles/201457107-Send-and-read-messages, read): "If you start writing a message and don't send it, it'll automatically save as a draft", listed under "Drafts & sent at the top of your sidebar".
  - Apple HIG *Modality* (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/modality, JSON read): "Regardless of whether people use a dismiss gesture or a button, if closing the view could result in the loss of user-generated content, be sure to explain the situation and give people ways to resolve it", for example "an action sheet that includes a save option". *Sheets*: "If people have unsaved changes in the sheet when they begin swiping to dismiss it, use an action sheet to let them confirm their action." Apple's confirmation applies only *when closing would lose content*. Autosaving the draft removes that condition.
  - WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.5 Re-authenticating (AAA, https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/re-authenticating.html, read): "When an authenticated session expires, the user can continue the activity without loss of data after re-authenticating." The same rule applied to a forced exit.
  - GOV.UK *Button* and *Task list* (https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/task-list/, via search result text only): explicit "Save and come back later" belongs to long transactional services, not to composers. **Not opened; keep it as context only.**
  - Lazar, Jones, Hackley & Shneiderman, *Severity and Impact of Computer User Frustration* (https://www.cs.umd.edu/~ben/papers/Lazar2006Severity.pdf, full text of the 2004 manuscript read): 107 students and 50 workplace users lost "1/3 to 1/2 of time spent" to frustrating incidents. Time lost (which included "making up for lost work"), time to fix and task importance were the only three variables that correlated strongly with frustration in both groups (p < .01). This is supporting evidence: it measures all frustrating incidents, not lost drafts specifically.
- **Rule for Track.**
  - The New issue composer writes its title, description and fields to `localStorage` on each change.
  - Click-outside, navigation, ⌘K, Esc and × all close the composer and keep the draft, with no question asked. Reopening with `C` restores it, cursor at the end.
  - A Discard command (a button in the composer footer and ⌘⌫) is the only way to drop the draft. It is undoable from a toast for a few seconds.
  - An inline comment box keeps its own draft per issue, the same way.
  - Deviation from Linear, which asks on Esc: the owner's rule is that a user-initiated action doesn't need a gate. Because nothing is lost, Apple's condition for confirming never arises.
- **Demo (one variable).** The same composer with half an issue typed. Only the close behaviour changes: discard on Esc, confirm on Esc, or keep the draft. A counter shows the characters lost across a session of switching between issues.
- **Verdict.** Page-worthy. The idea that the confirmation is the bug, not the cure, can be shown in a demo, and the Linear / Apple / Gmail behaviours give real cases. Boundary: *Error timing* covers when to validate, and this page covers what happens to input when the surface goes away.

## 3. Context menus and discoverability

- **Mechanism.** A context menu is a shortcut to commands that already exist, not their home. People who never right-click have to find every command elsewhere, and people who do right-click expect the menu to act on what they meant. The platforms disagree with Linear on one point, showing keys in the context menu, and the evidence sides with neither. *The faster way* already covers why a printed label teaches little (Grossman: 28.9% hotkey use). In ExposeHK's first study, hotkey use was 35% when the key appeared in tooltips, against 94% when it appeared on holding a modifier key (Malacria et al., CHI 2013, https://hal.science/hal-01894253/document, full text read; 36 participants; hotkey selections were 35% faster than pointer selections).
- **Mirror the screen.**
  - Apple HIG *Context menus* (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/context-menus, JSON read): "Always make context menu items available in the main interface, too." "Hide unavailable menu items, don't dim them" (Cut, Copy and Paste are the exception). "In general, you don't want more than about three groups in a context menu."
  - Windows *Menus* guidelines (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/uxguide/cmd-menus, full page read; written for Windows 7, "Much of the guidance still applies in principle"). "Don't make commands only available through context menus." "Don't put more than 15 items within a context menu." "Display the default command using bold" and put it first. Remove rather than disable items that don't apply, except standard ones such as Cut, Copy, Paste, Delete and Rename. The items "should apply only to the selected (or clicked upon) object".
- **Keys in the menu: the platforms disagree with Linear.**
  - Apple: "Show keyboard shortcuts in your app's main menus, not in context menus", because "it's redundant".
  - Windows: "Don't display shortcut key assignments within context menus. Context menus don't display the shortcut key assignments because they are optimized for efficiency."
  - Linear, *Invisible details* (https://linear.app/now/invisible-details, read): "contextual menus are also a great tool for onboarding and teaching people how to use our popular keyboard shortcuts."
  - What resolves it: a web app has no menu bar, which is where Apple sends people to learn the keys. So the context menu is the only list that is always one gesture away, and Linear's choice fills a gap the platforms don't have. Keep the keys in the menu, and record the deviation.
- **Selection vs the row under the pointer.**
  - Linear *Select issues* (https://linear.app/docs/select-issues, read): "right-click anywhere on the selected issue(s) to open the contextual menu"; "Highlighting or selecting one issue both allow you to take actions on the issue using keyboard shortcuts, the command menu, or right-click". "By default, no issue is selected". The doc doesn't say what happens when you right-click an unselected row while others are selected.
  - Apple names the multi-selection case only through the menu title: "a title that states the number of selected messages, reminding people that the command they choose affects all the messages they selected."
  - The Windows wording, "selected (or clicked upon)", covers both cases without settling the conflict. **No first-party rule for the conflict case was found.**
- **Rule for Track.**
  - Every context-menu item also exists in the side panel's toolbar or a row's ⋯ button.
  - Items that don't apply are hidden. Each row's menu has at most three groups, with the default action (Open) first and in bold.
  - Each item shows its key, as Linear does (recorded deviation from Apple and Windows, for the reason above).
  - Right-clicking inside the selection acts on all of it, and the menu header reads "3 issues".
  - Right-clicking a row outside the selection acts on that row only. The row gets a focus ring and the selection stays (proposed; test it in the demo, because it is the case nobody documents).
- **Demo (one variable).** Three rows selected. Only the outcome of right-clicking a fourth, unselected row changes: the selection grows, the selection is replaced, or only that row is targeted. The task: archive one issue without losing a selection that took effort to build.
- **Verdict.** Page-worthy as a *Context menu* variant on the existing *Menu* page (the backlog folded Context menu into existing variants). The new material is the selection-versus-pointer case and the platform-versus-Linear disagreement over showing keys. Don't repeat the hotkey numbers; link to *The faster way*.

## 4. First run and empty states

- **Mechanism.** In a new workspace every view is empty, so first use gives the product's structure nothing to show. Nobody can learn a grouped list, a board or a status flow from blank screens, and tours don't fill the gap. The largest controlled study found tutorials help only where the mechanics can't be found by trying them. A tracker's mechanics can be found by trying, as long as there is something to try them on.
- **Strongest finding.**
  - Andersen, O'Rourke, Liu, Snider, Lowdermilk, Truong, Cooper & Popović, *The Impact of Tutorials on Games of Varying Complexity*, CHI 2012 (https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf, full text read). A multivariate field experiment across three games, with over 45,000 players. In the complex game, tutorials "increased play time by as much as 29% and player progress by as much as 75%"; in the two simpler games they had "a surprisingly negligible effect". Giving instructions just when they were needed added 16% play time in the complex game and nothing elsewhere. Restricting freedom didn't help. On-demand help had "even negative effects" in one game. The authors conclude "investment in tutorials may not be justified for games that can be learned through experimentation". Caveat: these are games, not productivity apps. The study pairs with NN/g's 2023 tutorial article, already cited in the backlog.
  - Carroll & Carrithers, *Training Wheels in a User Interface*, CACM 27(8):800–806, 1984 (https://research.ibm.com/publications/training-wheels-in-a-user-interface, abstract read). Blocking the error states new users typically hit led to faster learning and better comprehension. The control group "spent almost a quarter of their time recovering from the error states" that the training interface blocked. The lesson for first run: remove the ways to get lost rather than explain them.
  - Apple HIG *Onboarding* (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding, JSON read): "Teach through interactivity"; "Consider providing a collection of context-specific tips instead of a single onboarding flow"; if a tutorial is skipped, "don't present it again on subsequent launches"; "Postpone nonessential setup flows or customization steps"; "Provide reasonable default settings".
  - Carbon *Empty states* (https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/empty-states-pattern/, read): the body should "Explain clearly the next action to populate the space". "Starter content can provide the opportunity for users to dive in and learn about primary features and functions with sample data". "If your starter content can be deleted by your user, you will need a basic empty state as a back up." Atlassian *Empty state* (https://atlassian.design/components/empty-state/usage, read): "Include a relevant call to action" but not "too many call to action buttons".
- **What reference products do.**
  - Notion: "When you first sign up for Notion, you'll see a collection of templates in your sidebar", chosen from onboarding answers, which "you can delete" (https://www.notion.com/help/start-with-a-template, read).
  - Linear: "When a workspace is created, Linear automatically creates a default Team with the same name as the workspace", and Enterprise admins can set a welcome message (https://linear.app/docs/workspaces, read). **No documentation was found that Linear seeds sample issues.**
- **Rule for Track.**
  - No tour and no coach marks.
  - A new workspace opens with one team and about a dozen sample issues spread across statuses. That's enough to fill the grouped list and the board, with a few already assigned to "you" so My issues isn't empty. A single row action deletes all the samples.
  - Every empty view says why it's empty and gives one next step with its key. Backlog: "No issues in Backlog. New issue `C`". My issues: "Nothing assigned to you. All issues `G A`".
  - The empty views a filter causes belong to *Filter bar* (backlog) and must read differently from "nothing exists yet".
- **Demo (one variable).** The same first-run workspace, changing only what's in it: empty with a five-step tour, or sample issues and no tour. Afterwards, ask "how do you move an issue to Done?".
- **Verdict.** Page-worthy as a principle. Andersen's "tutorials only pay where mechanics can't be discovered" is counterintuitive and has a 45,000-player sample. It absorbs the first-use empty state that the backlog explicitly left out of *Filter bar*.

## 5. Document and window titles reflect place

- **Mechanism.** The page title appears where the page itself isn't visible: the tab strip, the window switcher, the history list, bookmarks, and the first thing a screen reader announces. Most tab switches are *returns*, so the title works as a retrieval cue for a page someone already knows, not as a description for a newcomer. Tab strips truncate from the end, so only the first few words survive, and the specific part has to come first.
- **Strongest finding.**
  - Dubroy & Balakrishnan, *A Study of Tabbed Browsing Among Mozilla Firefox Users*, CHI 2010 (https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/ravin/papers/chi2010_tabbedbrowsing.pdf, full text read; 21 participants, 13–21 days of logs). Most participants "switched tabs more often than they used the back button". Back accounted for a median of 7.1% of navigation actions. "A median of 73.3% of tab switches were revisitations".
  - Obendorf, Weinreich, Herder & Mayer, *Web Page Revisitation Revisited*, CHI 2007 (https://vsis-www.informatik.uni-hamburg.de/getDoc.php/publications/280/chi2007-newformat.pdf, full text read). Back fell to 14.3% of navigation actions, from 36% in Catledge & Pitkow and 32% in Tauscher & Greenberg. "72.6% of all revisits" happened within an hour. "Only 3% of all back button events originated from this pull-down menu", because users found it simpler to click Back several times than to "scan its often incomprehensive list of page titles". Bad titles make the history list useless.
  - WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.2 Page Titled (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/page-titled.html, read): "Web pages have titles that describe topic or purpose". In single-page apps, "the title of the page should also be changed dynamically to reflect the content or topic of the current view".
  - GOV.UK *Writing for user interfaces* (https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/writing-for-user-interfaces, read): the title "should be based on the `<h1>`", format "Where do you live? - Register to vote - GOV.UK", page first, then service. GOV.UK *Error summary* (https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-summary/, read): add "Error: " to the start of the title "so screen readers read it out as soon as possible".
  - Apple HIG *Toolbars* (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/toolbars, JSON read): "A title helps people confirm their location as they navigate your app, and differentiates between the content of multiple open windows"; "Don't title windows with your app name"; keep it "under 15 characters". In-window titles drop the app name, but browser-tab titles keep it at the end (GOV.UK), because the tab strip mixes sites.
- **Rule for Track.**
  - Every view and every opened issue updates the URL (`pushState`) and `document.title`, most specific part first: `TRK-12 Fix login redirect · Track`, `Backlog · Design · Track`, `My issues · Track`, `Inbox (3) · Track`.
  - Opening the side panel is a place: it changes the URL and title. Closing the panel restores the view's title. Back closes the panel and returns to the list with its scroll and selection kept. That last behaviour is proposed and should be checked against Linear before claiming parity.
  - The in-app header repeats the same name as the `<h1>`, so the two never disagree.
- **Demo (one variable).** Eight Track tabs open in a fake tab strip of truncated width. Only the title format changes: `Track – Backlog – Design` vs `Backlog · Design · Track`. Task: switch to the Design backlog.
- **Verdict.** It shouldn't be a page on its own: WCAG and GOV.UK make the rule common knowledge. The counterintuitive part (most tab switches are returns, and only 3% of Back uses the history list because its titles can't be read) fits as a Detail on a future app-shell page, or under topic 1 as "the title is the sidebar's other half".

## 6. Notifications: Inbox vs inline updates

- **Mechanism.** One alert costs far more than the seconds it takes to read. People drift to other apps, then take as long again to get back into the task, and a quarter of interrupted tasks aren't resumed for hours. The answer isn't zero notifications: people who got none did *worse* than people whose notifications were batched. What works is delivering only what's addressed to the person, on a predictable rhythm, and at breaks in the task. A change to something already on screen needs no notification at all.
- **Strongest finding.**
  - Iqbal & Horvitz, *Disruption and Recovery of Computing Tasks*, CHI 2007 (https://www.erichorvitz.com/CHI_2007_Iqbal_Horvitz.pdf, full text read; 27 users, two weeks, logged in the field). 40.8% of email alerts and 71% of IM alerts got an immediate response (within 15 s). Diversions lasted 9 min 33 s (email) and 8 min (IM) before people returned to the suspended app. Getting back to the pre-interruption state took another 10–16 minutes. "27% of task suspensions resulted in more than two hours of time until resumption". Visible windows of the suspended task were resumed faster.
  - Iqbal & Bailey, *Effects of Intelligent Notification Management on Users and Their Tasks*, CHI 2008 (https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI08.pdf, abstract and setup read): "scheduling notifications at breakpoints reduces frustration and reaction time relative to delivering them immediately", and the relevance of the content decides which breakpoint suits it.
  - Mark, Gudith & Klocke, *The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress*, CHI 2008 (https://www.ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf, abstract via search only). Interrupted people finished faster with no loss of quality, but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. **Abstract only: use the direction of the finding, not numbers.**
  - Fitz, Kushlev, Jagannathan, Lewis, Paliwal & Ariely, *Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being*, Computers in Human Behavior 101:84–94, 2019 (https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1402953, abstract read). A randomized field experiment with 237 people, who got notifications as usual, batched three times a day, or never. The batched group "felt more attentive, productive" and reported lower stress. The never group "experienced higher levels of anxiety and 'fear of missing out'". (The abstract's "lower productivity" for the batched group contradicts its previous sentence. Treat it as a typo in the abstract and don't quote it.)
  - Apple HIG *Notifications* (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/notifications, JSON read). When the app is in front, present new information "in a way that's discoverable but not distracting or invasive, such as incrementing a badge or subtly inserting new data into the current view". Mail "simply adds it to the list of unread messages because sending a notification about it would be unnecessary and distracting". "Avoid sending multiple notifications for the same thing".
  - Linear *Inbox* (https://linear.app/docs/inbox, read): "You'll receive an Inbox notification for key events on your subscribed issues"; you're subscribed when you create an issue, are assigned to it or are mentioned. `U` toggles read; snooze "hides a notification from your Inbox until the selected time". Linear *Notifications* (https://linear.app/docs/notifications, read): desktop, mobile and Slack are "sent in real-time"; "Email digests send with time delays based on urgency"; "You'll always see notifications in your Linear inbox."
- **Rule for Track.**
  - Simulated teammate edits to visible issues update the row and the side panel in place. A new row is inserted without moving what's under the pointer (*Nothing shifts*), and there is no toast.
  - The Inbox collects only events on issues I created, am assigned to or am mentioned in, one entry per issue (newest event on top).
  - The sidebar Inbox row carries the unread count in the trailing-meta column (see `2026-10-27-colour-and-unread.md` for its colour). Read state is set by opening the entry, or with `U`.
  - While the composer or a comment box has focus, Inbox arrivals are held and land when it closes. That is the breakpoint rule.
  - Track shows no interrupting toast for teammate activity at all.
- **Demo (one variable).** A two-minute session of triaging Track while a script sends 12 teammate events. Only delivery changes: a toast per event, or in place plus the Inbox count. Measure triage completion and the number of times focus left the list.
- **Verdict.** Page-worthy as a principle, distinct from *Never too busy to listen* (motion interruption) and *Attention wears off, waiting doesn't* (habituation to motion). The surprise worth teaching: never notifying did worse than batching (Fitz), and an "innocuous" alert led to 10+ minutes of diversion plus 10+ minutes of getting back into the task (Iqbal & Horvitz).

## Ranked shortlist

1. **Interrupt only for me** (topic 6). It has the strongest numbers: 27% of alert-triggered suspensions stay unresumed for more than 2 hours, and recovery takes 10+ minutes on top of the diversion (Iqbal & Horvitz). It also has the most counterintuitive turn: no notifications did worse than batching (Fitz). It maps directly onto Track's teammate simulation and Inbox.
2. **Closing is not discarding** (topic 2). The demo is very easy to feel (type, press Esc, lose it). Linear, Gmail, Slack and Apple give real cases, and the claim that the confirmation is itself the problem is new to most readers.
3. **Start with something to look at** (topic 4). Andersen's 45,000-player result (tutorials help only where mechanics can't be discovered) and Carroll's blocked error states. It absorbs first-use empty states.
4. **Two levels, one toggle** (topic 1). Three-system consensus plus Fluent's move-the-mark-to-the-closed-group detail; best as an app-shell component page, with topic 5's title rule as a Detail.
5. **Right-click mirrors the screen** (topic 3), as a variant on *Menu*: the selection-versus-pointer case plus the platform-versus-Linear disagreement over keys. It should link to *The faster way*, not repeat it.
