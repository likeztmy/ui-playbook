# Icons: what an unlabelled glyph costs, and why a symbol character is not an icon

Research for the new component page **Icon**. The page's rules are already decided:
- icons come from one existing library, at one stroke weight, sized to the text beside them;
- emoji and text symbols (↗ ✓ ★ ✳ ×) never stand in for icons;
- no hand-drawn SVG;
- an icon-only control needs a name (tooltip plus `aria-label`).

This file collects the mechanisms and numbers behind those rules, from primary sources only.

Not repeated here:
- Target sizes (Apple, Material, Fluent, Carbon, Primer, WCAG 2.5.5 / 2.5.8) are in `2026-10-29-design-system-numbers.md`. §8 only cross-references them and adds what the icon pages say.

## 1. Without a label, an icon has to be learned, and what it depicts matters less than you'd think

- **Mechanism.** An icon's meaning isn't read off the picture. It's learned.
  - On first contact, people succeed when the picture is **semantically close** to the function (a printer for Print).
  - With use, **familiarity** takes over.
  - **Concreteness** (how realistic the drawing is) explains much less than designers assume.
  - The label does the work while the icon is being learned. That's why icon-only interfaces start badly and then catch up.
- **Classic.**
  - Wiedenbeck, *The use of icons and labels in an end user application program: an empirical study of learning and retention*, Behaviour & Information Technology 18(2):68–82, 1999, doi:10.1080/014492999119129 (**verified, abstract**, OpenAlex). Three versions of one program were compared: label-only, icon-only, and icon + label.
    - Session 1: performance "was best on the label-only and icon-label interfaces". Icon-only was "much poorer … particularly in terms of time and help references".
    - Session 2 (delayed): icon-only improved "to the point where it approached" the others.
    - Retention was worse for icon-only, "but the effect was short-lived".
    - Perceived ease of use was "consistently better for the icon-label interface".
    - The abstract gives no times, error rates or sample size. Those are **not found**.
  - McDougall, Curry & de Bruijn, *Measuring symbol and icon characteristics: Norms for concreteness, complexity, meaningfulness, familiarity, and semantic distance for 239 symbols*, Behavior Research Methods, Instruments & Computers 31(3):487–519, 1999, doi:10.3758/BF03200730 (**verified, abstract**). It defines the five dimensions everyone since has used. The abstract gives no effect sizes.
  - McDougall, de Bruijn & Curry, *Exploring the effects of icon characteristics on user performance: The role of icon concreteness, complexity, and distinctiveness*, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied 6(4):291–306, 2000, doi:10.1037/1076-898X.6.4.291 (**verified, abstract**).
    - Concreteness effects were "primarily associated with the initial grasp of meaning".
    - Complexity effects "persist longer" and are tied to search.
    - The abstract gives no numbers.
  - Isherwood, McDougall & Curry, *Icon identification in context: The changing role of icon characteristics with user experience*, Human Factors 49(3):465–476, 2007, doi:10.1518/001872007X200102 (**verified, abstract**).
    - 30 participants identified icons over a long series of trials.
    - Icon characteristics accounted for "up to 69% of the variance".
    - "Semantic distance is crucial initially". Familiarity matters later, with "lasting effects".
    - Concreteness "may not be of primary importance".
  - McDougall & Isherwood, *What's in a name? The role of graphics, functions, and their interrelationships in icon identification*, Behavior Research Methods 41(2):325–336, 2009, doi:10.3758/BRM.41.2.325 (**verified, abstract**, Springer page). In early trials, semantic distance accounted for "up to 55% of the variance". Familiarity with the *function* mattered more later.
- **Recent replications and extensions.**
  - Prada, Rodrigues, Silva & Garrido, *Lisbon Symbol Database (LSD): Subjective norms for 600 symbols*, Behavior Research Methods 48(4):1370–1382, 2016, doi:10.3758/s13428-015-0643-7 (**verified, abstract**, Springer page). This is a new norm set of 600 symbols rated by 388 participants on seven dimensions. "Overall, the dimensions were highly correlated", which is the McDougall confound again: realistic, simple and familiar tend to travel together.
  - Collaud, Reppa, Défayes, McDougall, Henchoz & Sonderegger, *Design standards for icons: The independent role of aesthetics, visual complexity and concreteness in icon design and icon understanding*, Displays 74:102290, 2022, doi:10.1016/j.displa.2022.102290 (**verified, abstract**). They built 64 icons that vary on one characteristic at a time to break that confound. The characteristics "had independent effects on icon processing, with two exceptions". The abstract gives no effect sizes.
  - Chajadi, Uddin & Gutwin, *Effects of Visual Distinctiveness on Learning and Retrieval in Icon Toolbars*, Graphics Interface 2020, doi:10.20380/GI2020.12 (**verified, full text**). There were two toolbar-learning studies with 20 participants each.
    - "no evidence that increasing colour or shape distinctiveness improved learning".
    - Concrete imagery did help. Visual characteristics "affect UI learnability much less than the meaning".
- **Older adults (where the label gap is widest).**
  - Dosso & Chevalier, *How do older adults process icons during a navigation task?*, Educational Gerontology 47(3):132–147, 2021, doi:10.1080/03601277.2021.1886634 (**verified, abstract**). On a tablet, the conditions were icon, text label, or both, with close or far semantic distance. Older users "needed more time to select icons, specifically when the semantic distance was far". There was no group difference in correct responses.
  - Wu et al., *How Do Older Adults Process Icons in Visual Search Tasks?*, IJERPH 19(8):4525, 2022, doi:10.3390/ijerph19084525 (**verified, abstract**). The study used 54 icons, flat or skeuomorphic, each with or without text, and eye tracking. All older adults "performed better at searching for the combinations of icon and text". They "performed poorly when searching for" flat icons alone.
  - Chen, Huang & Zhou, *Skeuomorphic or flat icons for an efficient visual search by younger and older adults?*, Applied Ergonomics 85:103073, 2020, doi:10.1016/j.apergo.2020.103073 (**verified, abstract**, Semantic Scholar). With 24 older and 24 younger participants, older ones searched skeuomorphic icons "more quickly and accurately". Participants' perceptions of icon style differed notably from designers'.
  - Song, Lin & Tang, *Developing icons for older adults: The roles of icon design categories, text labels, and learning strategies*, International Journal of Industrial Ergonomics 102:103613, 2024, doi:10.1016/j.ergon.2024.103613. **Secondary**: the abstract wasn't reachable (ScienceDirect blocked, none on Crossref or Semantic Scholar). A search snippet says text labels removed the age differences in comprehension.
- **Implications for the page.**
  - The label carries meaning on first use. The icon pays off only after it's familiar.
  - For a tool used daily, an icon-only control is fine for a few frequent actions **if the tooltip teaches the name**. The tooltip is how the label gets learned.
  - "Make the icon more realistic" is the weakest lever (Isherwood 2007, Chajadi 2020). Picking the conventional glyph, the one people already know, is the strongest. That's an argument for using a library's standard glyph over drawing a clever one.

## 2. Which icons are safe unlabelled: a short list set by convention, and navigation isn't on it

- **Mechanism.** "Universally recognised" is familiarity (§1), not good drawing. The safe set is the few glyphs every platform uses for the same action. Navigation is the case the evidence covers best, and that evidence says *hiding* labels costs use.
- **Recognition rates for specific UI glyphs (search, close, home, share, ☰).** **Not found.** No peer-reviewed study giving percent-correct for these as UI icons was found in this pass. The cross-cultural studies found (Swedish/US videophone symbols; Moroccan/Sri Lankan/Turkish/US web icons; Taiwanese browser icons) are small, use other icon sets, and were seen only as snippets. They're **secondary** and kept off pages.
- **Hidden navigation, measured (NN/g's own study).** Pernice & Budiu, *Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics*, Nielsen Norman Group, 2016-06-26 (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/, **verified**). Quantitative remote testing: 179 participants, 6 live sites, desktop and phone, 2 tasks per site, and participants weren't told to use the navigation.
  - Navigation use on desktop: hidden 27%, visible 48%, combo 50%. On mobile: hidden 57%, combo 86%.
  - Task difficulty (1–7 scale): hidden 2.6, combo 2.3, visible 2.1, so hidden was 21% harder than visible.
  - Task time: desktop hidden was at least 39% slower than visible or combo. Mobile hidden was 15% slower than combo.
  - Content discoverability dropped by more than 20% with hidden navigation.
  - **Limit**: this measures navigation *behind* an icon, not whether people recognise the ☰ glyph.
- **Older adults and ☰.** Li & Luximon, *Older adults' use of mobile device: usability challenges while navigating various interfaces*, Behaviour & Information Technology 39(8):837–861, 2020 (online 2019), doi:10.1080/0144929X.2019.1622786 (**verified, abstract**). 22 older adults did 19 navigation tasks. Participants had "great challenges" with "understanding the meaning of icons". The figure that 11 of the 22 couldn't understand the hamburger icon is **secondary** (a search snippet of the PDF).
- **Comprehension thresholds in standards.** ISO 9186's acceptance criterion for public-information symbols (often quoted as 66.7% or 67% correct) is **secondary**: it was seen only cited in a study snippet. ISO's Online Browsing Platform needs a terms-of-use acceptance, so it wasn't opened.
- **What the design systems allow unlabelled.**
  - **Apple HIG, *Toolbars*** (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/toolbars, JSON source read, **verified**).
    - For Back and Close: "Prefer the standard symbols for each, and don't use a text label".
    - "Prefer simple, recognizable symbols for items instead of text, except for actions like edit". Apple goes further toward icon-only than the others.
    - Its *Icons* page has a standard-icon table: Done = `checkmark`, Cancel = `xmark`, Delete = `trash`, Undo = `arrow.uturn.backward`, Compose = `square.and.pencil`, and others (**verified**).
  - **Material 3, *Applying icons*** (https://m3.material.io/styles/icons/applying-icons, rendered page read, **verified**).
    - "Remember that navigation items must have labels for clarity and accessibility".
    - "Use caution if icons are displayed without labels."
    - Below 20dp, complex icons and "Icons with a key action" need a text label. Simple symbols "like stars for ratings" can stand alone.
    - People who use the internet a lot "may have different understandings of icons".
  - **Primer, *IconButton*** (primer/design `content/components/icon-button.mdx`, **verified**).
    - Use it "when the purpose of your button may be easily understood using an icon".
    - "Consider your audience and the context". Always provide "a tooltip with a text label".
  - **Fluent 2 Nav**: "doesn't support an icon-only layout" (by reference, `2026-10-28-app-shell-and-guidance.md` §1).
- **Implications for the page.**
  - Unlabelled is safe only for a handful of conventional actions in their conventional place: close, back, search, more, and in an editor bold or italic. Even those get a tooltip.
  - Navigation always carries a label. That's Material's rule, Fluent's, and the NN/g numbers.
  - Apple's "prefer symbols in toolbars" is the honest counterweight. It works because Apple ships one standard glyph per action and every app reuses it, which is the familiarity mechanism from §1.

## 3. An emoji is the vendor's picture, and people read the same picture differently

- **Mechanism.**
  - Unicode encodes an emoji's identity, not its image. Each vendor draws its own, so the "same" character looks different on Apple, Google, Microsoft and Samsung.
  - The surprising part: most of the misreading happens **within** one rendering. Switching vendors adds only a little.
  - Emoji are ambiguous pictures before they're inconsistent ones.
- **The standard.** Unicode Technical Standard #51, *Unicode Emoji*, version 18.0, revision 31, 2026-08-28 (https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr51/, **verified**).
  - "The shape of the character can vary significantly."
  - The colour images in the standard "are for illustration only".
  - "presentation style is never guaranteed."
- **Classic.**
  - Miller, Thebault-Spieker, Chang, Johnson, Terveen & Hecht, *"Blissfully happy" or "ready to fight": Varying interpretations of emoji*, ICWSM 2016, pp. 259–268, doi:10.1609/icwsm.v10i1.14757 (**verified, full text**, author PDF). OpenAlex lists the year as 2021; the paper is 2016.
    - The method: 304 MTurk participants (334 recruited) rated the 25 most-tweeted face and people emoji, each as rendered by Apple, Google, Microsoft, Samsung and LG. Sentiment was rated on a −5 to 5 scale, and meaning was described in the participant's own words.
    - Same rendering: in 25% of rater pairs, the raters "did not agree on whether the sentiment was positive, neutral, or negative".
    - 40% of renderings (44 of 110) had an average sentiment disagreement of 2 or more points. Only 4.5% had 1 or less.
    - The worst case was Microsoft's "smiling face with open mouth and tightly closed eyes": 44% rated it negative and 54% positive.
    - Across platforms, 41% (9 of 22) of characters had a sentiment range wider than one unit. For U+1F601, Apple versus every other vendor scored above 4.7, while the others agreed among themselves (below 2).
    - **Within versus across**: average sentiment disagreement was 1.86 within a platform and 2.03 across platforms. Meaning disagreement was 0.84 versus 0.86. The cross-vendor penalty is real but small next to the baseline ambiguity.
- **Follow-ups.**
  - Miller, Kluver, Thebault-Spieker, Terveen & Hecht, *Understanding emoji ambiguity in context: The role of text in emoji-related miscommunication*, ICWSM 2017, pp. 152–161, doi:10.1609/icwsm.v11i1.14901 (**verified, abstract**). With 2,482 participants, surrounding text did not help. Misreading in context "appears to be roughly the same".
  - Tigwell & Flatla, *Oh that's what you meant!*, MobileHCI 2016 Adjunct, pp. 859–866, doi:10.1145/2957265.2961844 (**verified, abstract**). 436 people were surveyed, then rated emoji in a 2-D emotion space. There was variation "within and across platforms" (Android and iOS). The abstract gives no numbers.
  - Bai, Dan, Mu & Yang, *A Systematic Review of Emoji: Current Research and Future Perspectives*, Frontiers in Psychology 10:2221, 2019, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02221 (**verified, abstract**). It's a general review and adds no misreading numbers.
- **One code point, two looks (measured).** From Unicode `emoji-data.txt` (dated 2026-01-30) and `emoji-variation-sequences.txt`:
  - ✨ U+2728 has `Emoji_Presentation`, so it renders as a colour emoji by default.
  - ↗ U+2197, ✳ U+2733, ✔ U+2714, ✖ U+2716 and ⚙ U+2699 are `Emoji` **without** `Emoji_Presentation`. They default to a text glyph, but each has a VS16 sequence that turns it into a colour emoji.
  - ✓ U+2713, ★ U+2605, × U+00D7, ✕ U+2715 and → U+2192 aren't emoji at all. They're plain symbols drawn by whatever font has them (§5).
  - So "↗" can be a thin text arrow on one machine and a coloured tile on another, depending on an invisible selector and the font.
- **Implications for the page.**
  - An emoji in a control is a third party's drawing, in their colours, that changes per OS, and a quarter of people already disagree about it on the same OS.
  - The page's rule doesn't depend on the vendor difference. The baseline ambiguity alone (25%) is enough.
  - Faces were the study set. Object emoji like ✨ weren't measured, so don't quote 25% for them.

## 4. What a screen reader says: an icon is silent, a symbol character speaks its own name

- **Mechanism.**
  - A library icon is an SVG that's hidden from assistive tech, and the button gets a name you wrote.
  - A symbol character is **text**. It joins the button's accessible name and is spoken by whatever name the screen reader's dictionary gives it. That's often a Unicode or CLDR name nobody chose for your UI.
- **The names (measured from CLDR).** Source: `common/annotations/en.xml`, CLDR main branch, fetched 2026-10-29. These are the `type="tts"` short names:

  | Character | CLDR English TTS name | Unicode character name |
  |---|---|---|
  | ↗ U+2197 | "up-right arrow" | NORTH EAST ARROW |
  | ✨ U+2728 | "sparkles" | SPARKLES |
  | ✳ U+2733 | "eight-spoked asterisk" | EIGHT SPOKED ASTERISK |
  | × U+00D7 | "multiplication sign" | MULTIPLICATION SIGN |
  | ✔ U+2714 | "check mark" | HEAVY CHECK MARK |
  | ✖ U+2716 | "multiply" | HEAVY MULTIPLICATION X |
  | → U+2192 | "right-pointing arrow" | RIGHTWARDS ARROW |
  | ⭐ U+2B50 | "star" | WHITE MEDIUM STAR |
  | 🔍 U+1F50D | "magnifying glass tilted left" | LEFT-POINTING MAGNIFYING GLASS |
  | ▶ U+25B6 | "play button" | BLACK RIGHT-POINTING TRIANGLE |
  | ✓ U+2713 | *none in CLDR en* | CHECK MARK |
  | ★ U+2605 | *none in CLDR en* | BLACK STAR |
  | ✕ U+2715 | *none in CLDR en* | MULTIPLICATION X |
  | ☰ U+2630 | *none in CLDR en* | TRIGRAM FOR HEAVEN |

  - The brief's "north east arrow" is the Unicode *character name*. CLDR's spoken name is "up-right arrow". Which one a user hears depends on the screen reader.
  - The "hamburger" character ☰ is, by name, an I Ching trigram.
  - Unicode names come from `UnicodeData.txt` (**measured**).
- **Screen readers use this data.** NVDA 2026.3 User Guide (https://download.nvaccess.org/documentation/userGuide.html, **verified**).
  - The setting "Include Unicode Consortium data (including emoji) when processing characters and symbols" adds dictionaries from the Common Locale Data Repository.
  - A separate "Punctuation/Symbol Level" setting decides which symbols are spoken as words at all.
  - So the same button reads differently across screen readers and across one user's settings. What VoiceOver and JAWS speak for each character is **not verified**.
- **How the symbol joins the name.** *Accessible Name and Description Computation 1.2*, W3C Working Draft, 2026-10-21 (https://www.w3.org/TR/accname-1.2/, **verified**).
  - Hidden nodes, including `aria-hidden="true"`, are skipped: "Return the empty string."
  - A non-empty `aria-label` is used directly.
  - Otherwise the name is computed from content, and each text node contributes "its textual contents".
  - So `<a>Open ↗</a>` has the name "Open ↗". With NVDA's CLDR option on, it would be spoken roughly as "Open up-right arrow". That last step is **inference**, not tested in a screen reader this session.
- **WCAG 2.2** (https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/, **verified**).
  - *Non-text content* is defined as content "not expressing something in human language". Its note says "This includes ASCII art …, emoticons, leetspeak". A symbol used as an icon is non-text content even though it's a character.
  - SC 1.1.1, Controls: "it has a name that describes its purpose". For decoration: "implemented in a way that it can be ignored by assistive technology".
  - SC 4.1.2: "the name and role can be programmatically determined". *Understanding 4.1.2* lists the test rule "Image button has non-empty accessible name".
  - SC 2.5.3 Label in Name: "the name contains the text that is presented visually."
- ***Understanding 2.5.3*** (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/label-in-name.html, **verified**).
  - Symbolic text "should not be considered a visible label".
  - A ">" used "to mimic the appearance of the right-facing arrow" is "a symbol", not text.
  - The SC is "primarily aimed at users of speech recognition / voice control". Best practice: "have the text of the label at the start of the name."
  - Maths is the exception: "11×3=33" is a real label.
- **WAI Images tutorial, *Functional images*** (https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/functional/, **verified**). The alternative should "convey the action that will be initiated". The examples name the action: a printer icon gets "Print this page", and a magnifier button gets "Search".
- **What the libraries and systems do.**
  - **Primer IconButton** (**verified**).
    - The markup is a `<button aria-label="…">` with `<svg aria-hidden="true">`.
    - The tooltip "visually replicates the accessible name", appears on hover **and** on focus, and is dismissed with Esc.
    - The icon must be recognisable as its name, so that voice-control users can say "click search".
  - **Carbon icons** (carbon-website `src/pages/elements/icons/code.mdx`, **verified**): icons are treated as decorative and get `aria-hidden="true"` unless given `aria-label` or `aria-labelledby`.
  - **lucide-react 1.61.0** (installed in this repo, **measured** from `dist/esm/shared/src/build/buildLucideIconNode.mjs` and `utils/hasA11yProp.mjs`): it adds `aria-hidden="true"` automatically unless the icon gets an `aria-*` prop, `role`, `title` or children. So the library's default is already the right one, and the name belongs on the button.
  - **Material 3, *Icon buttons › Accessibility*** (https://m3.material.io/components/icon-buttons/accessibility, **verified**).
    - The label "describes the action", for example "Add to favorites" for a heart.
    - "On web, icon buttons should display a tooltip with an accessibility label."
  - **Apple HIG, *Icons***: "Provide alternative text labels for custom interface icons" (**verified**).
- **Implications for the page.**
  - With a library icon, the author decides what's spoken: the icon is silent and the button's `aria-label` names the action.
  - With a symbol character, Unicode, CLDR and the user's screen-reader settings decide. Every "Open ↗" link may announce an arrow, and a ✓ may announce nothing, "check mark" or something else.
  - The tooltip and the `aria-label` should be the same words. Primer builds them from one prop, which also satisfies 2.5.3 for voice control.

## 5. A symbol the text font lacks is drawn by a font you didn't choose

- **Mechanism.**
  - CSS picks a font **per character**. When none of the listed families has a glyph, the browser asks the OS for any installed font that does.
  - That font has its own weight, x-height, side bearings and baseline. The symbol arrives at a different weight from the text around it, and differently on every OS.
- **The spec.** *CSS Fonts Module Level 4*, W3C Working Draft, 2026-10-22, §5 Font matching algorithm (https://www.w3.org/TR/css-fonts-4/, **verified**).
  - If the matched face lacks the glyph, "the next family name is selected". Once the list is exhausted, the UA "performs an installed font fallback procedure". "The result of this procedure can vary across user agents."
  - If no font has the glyph, the UA shows a missing-glyph symbol, for example from a "Last Resort Font".
  - On `font-size-adjust`: "fallback fonts might not share the same aspect value", and so are "less readable".
  - `font-variant-emoji` (`normal | text | emoji | unicode`) sets a default presentation. VS15/VS16 in the text override it, and its interaction with fallback "is explicitly unspecified".
- **In this repo (measured).** The `@fontsource-variable/geist` and `@fontsource-variable/space-grotesk` woff2 subsets shipped here (latin, latin-ext, cyrillic, cyrillic-ext, vietnamese) contain **×** but **none** of ↗ ✓ ✔ ★ ☆ ✳ ✕ → ✨ ☰ (cmap checked with fontTools). In Track or Post, every one of those characters is drawn by a system fallback font the page doesn't control. Which font macOS, Windows or Android picks is **not measured**.
- **Evidence that mixed fonts read worse.** No study of UI symbols drawn by fallback was found (**not found**). The closest mechanism is "font tuning" in letter recognition:
  - Sanocki, *Font regularity constraints on the process of letter recognition*, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 14(3):472–480, 1988, doi:10.1037/0096-1523.14.3.472 (**verified, abstract**).
  - Four-letter strings in one font were identified more accurately than strings that mixed two fonts. The advantage "increased at a faster rate as a function of processing time" and held across four sessions.
  - The explanation is a perceptual system "tuned to the regularities of a particular font".
  - Applying this to icons next to text is **inference**.
- **Implications for the page.**
  - "Just type ✓" means "let the OS pick a font for this one character". The weight, size and baseline then belong to that font, not to the type system.
  - An icon library's SVG is drawn at a stroke you chose (§6) on every platform.
  - × is the one symbol the shipped fonts do contain. It's still the multiplication sign ("multiplication sign" in §4), not a close icon.

## 6. Size and stroke: every system ties the icon to the text it sits beside

- **Mechanism.** An icon next to a label reads as part of the line when its **height** tracks the text size and its **stroke** tracks the text's stem weight. The systems do this in three ways:
  - Apple and Material through weight axes;
  - Carbon, Primer and Fluent through fixed size pairs;
  - Lucide through a stroke that scales with the icon.
- **Apple, SF Symbols** (https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols, JSON source read, change log "July 28, 2025", **verified**).
  - "Each of the nine symbol weights" (ultralight to black) "corresponds to a weight of the San Francisco system font".
  - There are three scales, small, medium (default) and large, defined "relative to the cap height". Scale changes emphasis "without disrupting the weight matching".
  - The outline variant has "no solid areas, resembling the appearance of text", and suits places "alongside text".
  - From *Icons* (**verified**): "match the weights of interface icons and adjacent text", and "use the same stroke weight in every icon."
- **Material Symbols** (https://m3.material.io/styles/icons/applying-icons and /designing-icons, rendered pages read, **verified**; axis defaults **measured** from `MaterialSymbolsOutlined[FILL,GRAD,opsz,wght].ttf`, google/material-design-icons):
  - **Axes**:
    - FILL 0–1 (default 0);
    - wght 100–700 (default 400);
    - GRAD −50 to 200 (default 0);
    - opsz 20–48 (default 24);
    - units per em 960.
  - **Sizes**: 24dp standard. 20dp is "primarily for desktop, dense layouts", and 40 and 48dp are for display or headline type.
  - **Grid**: a 20 × 20dp live area with 2dp padding. The recommended stroke is "2dp or the regular weight (400)".
  - **Weight**: "Don't use the lightest weight for standard-size (24dp) icons". The minimum at that size is 200, and "Don't mix different weights".
  - **With text**: "Use the same size for your Material Symbols and text" and "the same optical weight". Shift the symbol's baseline down "approximately 11.5% of the text size".
  - **Grade**: it can match a text font's grade (both −25). A light icon on a dark background defaults to −25 to offset visual bleed, and a positive grade marks an active state.
  - **Optical size**: it keeps strokes from getting "too heavy" when a 24dp source is scaled up.
- **Fluent 2, *Iconography*** (https://fluent2.microsoft.design/iconography, rendered page read, **verified**).
  - "Use 12 pixel icons to give information". They're "generally too small for interactions".
  - "The more precise the tool at hand, the smaller the icon can be."
  - Stroke and text pairing: **not found** on the page.
  - **Measured** from microsoft/fluentui-system-icons (`assets/Add/SVG`): `add` ships at 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32 and 48, regular and filled, plus a 32 light. The regular bar width is 1 px at 16 and 20, and 1.5 px at 24.
- **Carbon, *Icons › Usage*** (carbon-website `src/pages/elements/icons/usage.mdx`, **verified**).
  - Icons are "typically" on 16px artboards, with 20, 24 and 32 also available.
  - "16px and 20px icons are optimized to feel balanced when paired with 14px and 16px IBM Plex." "Don't alter the icon-text size ratio."
  - Center-align icons next to text and "Don't baseline-align" them. "Do match your icon color with your text color".
  - **Measured**: `packages/icons/src/svg/32/add.svg` draws a 2 px bar on the 32 artboard, which is 1 px when shown at 16. Carbon's own 16 px artboard wasn't fetched (404 at that path).
- **Primer, Octicons design guidelines** (primer/design `content/foundations/icons/design-guidelines.mdx`, **verified**).
  - "Always design two versions of each icon: a 16px version and a 24px version". 12 px is for special cases only.
  - "Use a consistent stroke width of 1.5px for both 16px and 24px icons." Corners take a 1 px radius.
  - **Measured**: `plus-16.svg` draws a 1.5 px bar.
- **Lucide, *Icon design specification*** (https://lucide.dev/contribute/icons/specification; source `docs/contribute/icons/specification.md`, **verified**).
  - It "must use a 24 × 24-pixel canvas".
  - "Strokes must be 2 pixels wide", with round joins and caps, at least 1 px from the edge, and at least 2 px between elements.
  - Its visual weight should be "similar to the circle and square icons".
  - The design-principles page adds: "Don't use thicker or thinner strokes, or mix stroke widths."
  - **Measured** in lucide-react 1.61.0 (`defaultAttributes.mjs`, `Icon.mjs`): the defaults are `size` 24 and `stroke-width` 2 on a `0 0 24 24` viewBox, so the stroke **scales with size**. `absoluteStrokeWidth` rescales it to stay 2 px on screen, and `nonScalingStroke` sets `vector-effect: non-scaling-stroke`.
- **Lucide's stroke against Geist's stems (computed).**
  - Lucide's rendered stroke is 2 × size / 24: 1.17 px at 14, **1.33 px at 16**, 1.67 px at 20 and 2.00 px at 24.
  - The stem of Geist's capital I, from the installed `geist-latin-wght-normal.woff2` instanced with fontTools (units per em 1000):
    - wght 400: 86 units, so **1.20 px at 14** and **1.38 px at 16**;
    - wght 500: 108 units, so 1.51 px and 1.73 px;
    - wght 600: 130 units, so 1.82 px and 2.08 px.
  - A Lucide icon sized to the text therefore lands within about 0.05 px of Geist Regular's stem at 14 and 16.
  - Two choices break that match:
    - The default 24 px icon beside 14 px text has a 2.0 px stroke against a 1.2 px stem, **1.7× heavier**.
    - `absoluteStrokeWidth` at 16 px keeps a 2 px stroke, which matches Geist **SemiBold** (2.08), not Regular.
- **Implications for the page.**
  - "Sized to the text" isn't only about height. With Lucide's scaling stroke, it is also the weight match Apple and Material build axes for.
  - Leave the stroke at Lucide's default and **don't** set `absoluteStrokeWidth`, or icons go bold next to regular text.
  - Carbon gives the pairing in so many words: 16 and 20 px icons go with 14 and 16 px text. Material gives "same size as text" plus an 11.5% baseline drop. Carbon says centre, don't baseline-align. Pick one alignment rule and check it against the reference product.

## 7. One family, one style; filled means selected

- **Mechanism.**
  - A set reads as one system when every icon shares a grid, stroke, corner and level of detail.
  - Fill is then free to carry **state**. Outline is the resting form, and filled is the selected form.
  - Mixing families spends that channel on noise. A filled icon from family B looks "selected" next to outline icons from family A.
- **Design-system guidance (all verified, sources as in §6).**
  - **Apple *Icons***: all icons "need to use a consistent size, level of detail, stroke thickness (or weight), and perspective", "whether you use only custom icons or mix custom and system-provided ones".
  - **Material 3 *Designing icons***: "Use and maintain a consistent visual style throughout one icon set" and "Avoid mixing styles for one icon set". Outlined, Rounded and Sharp are three styles to choose *one* of, matched to the brand's shapes and type.
  - **Lucide**: "a consistent icon set, not a collection of unrelated icons". New icons should reuse "established geometry".
  - **Carbon**: "Be sure icon size is consistent throughout your product."
- **Fill as state.**
  - **Apple SF Symbols**: use "the fill variant to indicate selection". "an iOS tab bar prefers the fill variant, whereas a toolbar takes the outline variant." From *Icons*: standard toolbars, tab bars and buttons update the selected state "automatically", so don't draw your own.
  - **Material 3**: "A fill attribute can be used to convey a state of transition". The example is "Bottom navigation with filled symbols in selected and unselected states". A positive grade marks "an active icon state".
  - **Fluent 2**: Regular is "a wayfinding device". Filled is "for highlighting selected states".
- **Evidence.**
  - **Not found**: no peer-reviewed study that tests a mixed-family icon set against a single family.
  - The nearest measured result is a **counterweight**. Chajadi, Uddin & Gutwin 2020 (§1) made icons *more* distinct in colour and shape and found no learning benefit, while meaning helped. So the consistency the systems ask for doesn't cost learnability, and the meaning carries the learning.
  - Sanocki 1988 (§5) is the perceptual analogy for why a mixed set reads as less regular. Applying it to icons is **inference**.
  - **Outline versus filled speed**: Arledge, a 2014 UNC master's paper (1,260 web participants), found no style "objectively better". It's **secondary** (search snippets) and not peer-reviewed, so keep it off pages.
- **Implications for the page.**
  - One library is a rule about **meaning**, not taste. Once fill means "selected", only a single family keeps that signal clean.
  - The reasons to state are the systems' own consistency rules and the state channel. Don't claim a measured speed cost for mixing, because none was found.

## 8. Glyph size versus target size (cross-reference)

- The numbers are in `2026-10-29-design-system-numbers.md`:
  - Apple iOS: 44 × 44 pt default, 28 × 28 pt minimum.
  - macOS: 28 × 28 pt default, 20 × 20 pt minimum.
  - Material: 48 × 48dp touch, 44 × 44dp pointer.
  - Fluent web/iOS: 44, Android: 48.
  - Primer: 16 fine pointer, 44 coarse pointer.
  - WCAG 2.5.8 (AA): 24 × 24 CSS px. WCAG 2.5.5 (AAA): 44 × 44.
- **What the icon pages add (verified this session).**
  - **Material 3 *Applying icons***: "Symbols of 24dp should have a target size of 48dp by default." With a mouse and keyboard, "A 20dp size symbol can use a target size of 40dp."
  - **Material 3 *Icon buttons › Accessibility***: the target is "at least 48dp, even when nested". "Don't apply density to icon buttons by default". The icon needs 3:1 contrast with its surface.
  - **Carbon *Usage***: interactive icons need targets of "44px or larger", padded with CSS. The example is "A 22px icon centered in a 48px touch target."
  - **Fluent 2**: 12 px icons are "generally too small for interactions".
- **Implication for the page.** The glyph follows the text (16 or 20 px). The **button** follows the target table. The padding between them belongs to the button, never to a bigger icon.

## Strongest counterintuitive points for the page

1. **Realism is the weakest lever; convention is the strongest.**
   - Isherwood et al. 2007: icon characteristics explain up to 69% of identification variance. Semantic distance leads early, familiarity later, and concreteness "may not be of primary importance".
   - McDougall & Isherwood 2009: semantic distance alone explains up to 55% early on.
   - Chajadi et al. 2020: extra colour or shape distinctiveness gave no learning gain (2 × 20 participants).
   - So use the library's standard glyph, not a clever new drawing.
2. **Icon-only starts badly and catches up, so the tooltip is the label being learned** (Wiedenbeck 1999). Icon-only was "much poorer" in session 1 and approached the labelled versions by session 2. Icon + label was rated easiest to use throughout.
3. **Hidden behind an icon, navigation gets used half as often on desktop** (NN/g's own study, Pernice & Budiu 2016, N = 179). Navigation use: 27% hidden vs 48% visible. Tasks were at least 39% slower on desktop. Material: "navigation items must have labels".
4. **Emoji are misread even when nobody changes phones** (Miller et al. 2016, N = 304).
   - In 25% of pairs looking at the *same* rendering, raters disagreed on whether it was positive, neutral or negative.
   - Cross-vendor disagreement is only a little higher: 2.03 vs 1.86 on a −5..5 scale.
   - Text around the emoji doesn't fix it (Miller et al. 2017, N = 2,482).
5. **A symbol character is spoken by its Unicode or CLDR name.**
   - ↗ is "up-right arrow" in CLDR (Unicode: NORTH EAST ARROW), ✨ is "sparkles", and ✳ is "eight-spoked asterisk".
   - The hamburger ☰ is named TRIGRAM FOR HEAVEN, and ✓ and ★ have no CLDR English name at all.
   - Per accname 1.2, the character joins the button's accessible name. *Understanding 2.5.3* says a ">" arrow "is a symbol", not a label.
6. **In this repo, every one of those symbols falls back to a font you didn't pick.** The shipped Geist and Space Grotesk subsets contain × but none of ↗ ✓ ★ ✳ → ✨ (measured). CSS Fonts 4: the fallback "can vary across user agents". Sanocki 1988: letters mixed from two fonts are recognised less accurately than single-font strings.
7. **Sized to the text, Lucide already matches the text's weight, and the "keep it 2 px" option breaks it** (computed).
   - At 16 px, Lucide's stroke is 1.33 px against Geist Regular's 1.38 px stem.
   - A default 24 px icon beside 14 px text is 1.7× heavier (2.0 vs 1.2 px).
   - `absoluteStrokeWidth` at 16 px matches Geist SemiBold (2.08).
   - Apple and Material solve the same problem with nine weights and a grade axis. Carbon pairs 16 and 20 px icons with 14 and 16 px text.
